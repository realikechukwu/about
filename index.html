Clinical tools

Most of what I build starts as a friction I ran into on the ward, in clinic or in my day-to-day life.

Clinote: a logbook you can talk to

You describe a procedure or a case in your own words. Clinote turns it into a structured logbook entry. You read it, fix anything that's wrong, and save it. Nothing is saved until you've checked it. I designed and built it end to end, and I'm constantly iterating on it.

The Cardiology Digest

An email that goes out every Sunday with the week's new cardiology papers worth reading. It checks about 20 journals, filters on the strength of the evidence, and summarises each paper in plain clinical terms. Summaries come from the abstract only. My LinkedIn post announcing it was very popular and drew a lot of interest. That showed me how useful it was and got me thinking about expanding it further down the line.

MedPally: keeping up with the evidence

MedPally grew out of the Cardiology Digest. You pick a specialty and the journals you care about, and it gives you a personal feed of new papers, each with a short summary. It pulls from PubMed on a schedule and works out which papers are relevant to you. Cardiology is the main focus, but at the request of colleagues it now also covers general practice and spine surgery, and it can be expanded to other specialties.

Django, htmx, PubMed, LLM summaries

MedPally Find

A free, hand-curated directory of tools, apps, courses and guidelines for cardiology and cardiac critical care. Most are educational tools built by clinicians, and about 50 resources are currently listed. Anyone can suggest a resource, and every suggestion is reviewed before it goes up. The code is open source under the MIT licence.

AI in Cardiology Decoded

A free, plain-language guide to machine learning for clinicians. I built it after I found this really good paper but struggled to understand a lot of it and needed a lot of AI assistance to explain the concepts. After getting AI to explain and break it down so clearly, I thought it would be reasonable to share it. It takes you from the basics to more advanced topics, and it simplifies complex concepts into digestible pieces. You can start with what a decision tree is and grow your understanding all the way to Transformers. It's an independent study companion to the 2026 ESC scientific statement on AI in cardiology for non-experts.

How I build

  • After I decide on a problem, I go to Apple Notes and use it as a scratch pad to write out my problem statement. Sometimes I dictate it into a voice memo instead.
  • Then I describe in clear terms what I want the solution to do and the exact stack I want it built on. I also describe features that make it easy to edit on the go.
  • Next, I iteratively prompt an AI coding agent to get a working plan.
  • I try to make sure I review as much as I can before the writing starts and I tend to feed it a lot of visual inspiration and hand sketches for my UI work.
  • I tend to use a lot of flow charts and HTML artefacts to review the output as it makes it easier to spot issues.
  • A lot of the time, I bring in skills and open-source GitHub repos in post-production to clean up the UI and copy. I used to write my own skills for this, but I realised some people do it better, so now I copy their skills and edit them to fit my workflow.
  • The tools I use are VS Code as my editor, Claude Code, Codex, GitHub and Cloudflare. If I have a big job, I hand it to Codex on the command line, as it burns fewer tokens. I also work remotely from my phone, using remote control for the tools.
  • For things I do over and over, like turning a PDF into Markdown, I write scripts inside my skills so they don't burn through my tokens.
  • GitHub Issues has come in very handy and I find that it really helps me keep on top of my work despite my busy schedule.
  • Cloudflare Workers has become an essential part of my workflow, allowing me to deploy and manage a lot of my applications with ease.

Medicine and research

Medicine is the day job behind all of this. I trained at the University of Ibadan, came to the UK through PLAB, and hold the MRCP(UK). I've worked in the NHS for five years across various medical and surgical specialties, and I'm now a cardiology registrar in Merseyside.

Alongside clinical work, I was an NIHR Academic Clinical Fellow working on cardiovascular outcomes and inequalities using real-world NHS data. I enjoy evidence synthesis and critical appraisal, and I use Python and R to drive my data analysis.
